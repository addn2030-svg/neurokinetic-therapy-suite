What NeuroKinetic Therapy Is (and Isn't)

NeuroKinetic Therapy® (NKT®) is a manual assessment and corrective-movement system developed by David Weinstock (a 1973 Johns Hopkins pre-med graduate practicing manual therapy since the 1970s). It is built on Motor Control Theory: the idea that the cerebellum's Motor Control Center (MCC) stores learned movement "programs," and that after injury, pain, or repetitive strain, the MCC substitutes a compensation pattern to keep the movement goal achievable. Tissue can heal completely while the compensation program remains permanently stored — NKT's core premise is that manual muscle testing (MMT) can reveal that stored program, and a structured test → release → retest → activate protocol can reprogram it.

NKT is not a diagnostic substitute for orthopedic/neurological exam, imaging, or medical clearance, and it is not a stand-alone treatment modality — it is a functional map that tells a practitioner where to apply whatever release technique (soft tissue work, IASTM, dry needling, stretching, taping) they are already trained in, followed by targeted activation exercise.

Level 1 — Fundamentals

The Motor Control Hierarchy

NKT frames dysfunction as a hierarchy problem, from cognitive intent down to the muscle fiber:

  1. Cerebral Cortex — conscious movement intent and strategic planning.
  2. Limbic System — emotional/threat appraisal; fear-avoidance and protective guarding can prime a pattern before any tissue is even injured.
  3. Cerebellum (Motor Control Center, MCC) — stores and automates multi-joint "motor engrams." It learns strictly through trial, error, and physical failure (the classic example: an infant learning to stand only succeeds after many failed attempts are filtered out).
  4. Basal Ganglia & Brainstem — postural tone and automatic anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs).
  5. Spinal Cord & Peripheral System — alpha/gamma motor neurons, muscle spindles (Ia/II afferents), Golgi tendon organs (Ib afferents), and joint mechanoreceptors that report real-time state back up the chain.
The Rehabilitation Catch-22: Histological healing of an injured tissue does not automatically erase the compensation engram the MCC built while protecting that tissue. The faulty program can persist for years after full tissue healing — this is why patients "feel fine on the table" but re-injure the same pattern under load.

Manual Muscle Testing (MMT): Mechanics & Grading

Contact Pressure Categories

PressureDurationPurpose
Light1–2 secondsTests availability of the neural pathway (can the muscle be recruited at all?)
Moderate1–2 secondsStandard clinical strength test used for most NKT screening
Heavy5–10 seconds, firm pressureTests stamina / fatigability of the motor unit pool under sustained load

Binary Classification (Neurological, not Orthopedic)

NKT deliberately uses a binary neurological outcome instead of the 0–5 Kendall/Daniels orthopedic grading scale, because it is testing rate-coding and recruitment latency, not raw contractile strength:

ResultFeelInterpretation
FacilitatedCrisp, rigid, immediate "lock"Muscle is neurologically overactive / available for recruitment (may still be tight/short)
InhibitedSpongy "give," delayed recruitment, or joint bucklesMuscle is neurologically underactive / not being called upon by the MCC in this pattern (may still be strong in isolation elsewhere)

Core Execution Rules

  • Contraction type: strictly isometric — the patient matches the examiner's force; no joint motion is allowed.
  • Starting position: shortened / inner-to-mid range, to minimize passive non-contractile tension confounding the result.
  • Force ramp: a smooth ~2-second ramp from 0% to 100% (roughly 10–15 lb of examiner force) — never a jerked or ballistic push.
  • Vector alignment: the resistance vector must exactly match the muscle's anatomical line of pull; any drift invites substitution from a neighboring muscle and produces a false result.
  • Directional Testing: testing the same joint through several resisted directions (not just the single "textbook" vector) to expose exactly which fibers/synergists are substituting.

Testing & Terminology Foundations

TermMeaning

Reactive (Muscle) PairTwo muscles linked in a kinetic chain such that releasing/inhibiting one immediately restores strength in the other on retest.

Compensation Taxonomy: The Two Level-1 Patterns

1. Synergistic Dominance

When a prime mover is inhibited, a synergist that normally assists the same movement takes over and becomes chronically overworked (facilitated), while the true prime mover stays inhibited. This is an agonist-substitutes-for-agonist problem within the same movement pattern.

Dominant Synergist (Facilitated)True Prime Mover (Inhibited)Common Clinical Picture
Tensor Fasciae Latae (TFL)Gluteus MediusIT band friction syndrome, greater trochanteric pain, Trendelenburg-type gait faults
Hamstrings / Erector SpinaeGluteus MaximusHamstring strains, lumbar facet overload, "hamstring-dominant" hip extension
Upper Trapezius / Levator ScapulaeLower Trapezius / Serratus AnteriorChronic neck/shoulder spasm, subacromial impingement, scapular winging
Piriformis / Deep RotatorsGluteus Maximus (extension) / Gluteus Medius (abduction)Deep gluteal (piriformis-type) pain, posterior hip tightness
Rectus FemorisIliopsoasAnterior hip/knee pain, quad-dominant hip flexion strategy
Biceps Brachii / Upper Trap ShrugRotator Cuff (Infraspinatus/Teres Minor)Shoulder instability masked by shrug-and-pull substitution during overhead reach

2. Reciprocal Inhibition

Per Sherrington's Law of Reciprocal Innervation, a chronically tight/overactive muscle reflexively inhibits its direct antagonist across the joint. This is an antagonist-inhibits-agonist problem.

Overactive Driver (Facilitated / Tight)Reflexively Inhibited AntagonistCommon Clinical Picture

Clinical distinction: Synergistic dominance is confirmed by testing the prime mover in isolation while the dominant synergist is neutralized/stabilized; reciprocal inhibition is confirmed by releasing the tight antagonist and immediately retesting the inhibited agonist within the neuroplastic window.
Level 2 — Advanced

The Four Kinetic Subsystems

Level 2 extends single-muscle testing to whole-body force-transfer "slings," conceptually aligned with the integrated core/kinetic-chain models used across manual therapy (Vleeming, Myers) but tested here through NKT's facilitated/inhibited MMT lens.

Posterior Oblique Subsystem (POS)

Chain: Latissimus Dorsi ↔ Thoracolumbar Fascia ↔ Contralateral Gluteus Maximus.

Function: Transfers rotational/propulsive force diagonally across the trunk during gait (e.g., right lat loads through the fascia into the left glute max during arm-swing/leg-drive coupling) and helps compress and stabilize the sacroiliac joint. Assessed with cross-body gait-pattern muscle tests (contralateral shoulder extension + hip extension).

Anterior Oblique Subsystem (AOS)

Chain: External Oblique ↔ Linea Alba/Anterior Abdominal Fascia ↔ Contralateral Internal Oblique & Adductor Complex.

Function: Rotational trunk stability for cutting, kicking, and swinging motions. Dysfunction here is classically implicated in athletic pubalgia ("sports hernia") and groin-adductor overload.

Deep Longitudinal Subsystem (DLS)

Chain: Erector Spinae ↔ Sacrotuberous Ligament ↔ Biceps Femoris ↔ Peroneus Longus.

Function: Sagittal-plane force transmission from the ground (heel strike) up through the leg, pelvis, and spine, using the sacrotuberous ligament as a key tension link between hamstring and erector spinae fascia.

Lateral Subsystem

Chain: Gluteus Medius ↔ TFL ↔ Contralateral Quadratus Lumborum & Adductors.

Function: Frontal-plane pelvic stability during single-limb stance (prevents a Trendelenburg drop). Tested with single-leg-stance indicator patterns and hip-hike/adduction challenges.

Core Cylinder, Diaphragm & Breath Mechanics

The diaphragm is treated as a central "core" muscle because of its direct fascial and functional links to the thoracic/lumbar erectors, quadratus lumborum, psoas, abdominals, pelvic floor, and — via the Valsalva mechanism — even the jaw.

Inhalation vs. Exhalation Breath-Hold Testing

An indicator muscle is manually tested twice: once while the patient holds their breath in, and once while they hold their breath out. A muscle that is strong in one breath state and inhibited in the other implicates the diaphragm/respiratory pattern as a driver.

Documented clinical pattern (chronically facilitated/overactive diaphragm): commonly correlates with inhibition of the deep neck flexors, pectoralis major (bilateral), humeral flexors/extensors (bilateral), psoas (bilateral), rectus femoris (bilateral), gluteus maximus (bilateral), and several contralateral gait patterns — reflecting how a single overburdened core muscle can radiate dysfunction through the entire kinetic chain.

Zone of Apposition (ZOA)

The area where the dome of the diaphragm directly opposes the inner rib cage. A well-restored ZOA supports optimal diaphragm length-tension and efficient intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) generation for spinal stabilization; subcostal manual release techniques aim to restore this apposition before re-testing core indicator muscles.

Intrinsic Core ("Inner Unit") & Diastasis Recti

Level 2 study material also covers spotting a loss of intrinsic/inner-unit core control (transversus abdominis, multifidus, pelvic floor, diaphragm co-contraction) and assessing diastasis recti as a driver of superficial rectus abdominis or oblique inhibition, particularly postpartum.

Joint Compression & Decompression Reflexes

Joint capsule mechanoreceptors respond differently to compressive vs. distractive load. NKT Level 2 introduces testing an indicator muscle under manual joint compression versus manual joint decompression/traction (classically demonstrated at the fingers, wrist, and hip) to determine whether a joint's mechanoreceptor signaling — rather than a muscle-to-muscle relationship — is driving the inhibition. This is clinically relevant for conditions like hammer toes, early joint arthrosis, or post-immobilization stiffness.

TMJ / Stomatognathic System

The jaw is tested as a core-adjacent structure due to shared fascial, developmental, and neurological (trigeminal/vagal) connections to the cervical spine, pelvic floor, and diaphragm.

  • Primary jaw muscles tested: Masseter, Temporalis, Medial & Lateral Pterygoids, Digastric.
  • Accessory muscles of jaw movement: assessed for their contribution to bite-pattern compensation.
  • Overbite/underbite global testing: checking how a structural bite abnormality changes whole-body indicator muscle results (a signature "global test" concept in NKT).
  • Common driver-receiver relationship: Masseter/Temporalis/Pterygoid overactivity co-presenting with deep neck flexor, pelvic floor, or core stabilizer inhibition.

Perturbation & Anticipatory Postural Adjustments (APAs)

Standing rotational or multi-directional perturbation challenges are used to assess how well the basal ganglia/brainstem generate feed-forward postural bracing before a destabilizing force arrives — exposing subsystems that fail specifically under dynamic/reactive load even when they test fine in static, isolated MMT.

Level 3 — Masters

Muscle-to-Muscle Testing & Refined Protocol

Where Levels 1–2 primarily use therapy localization (a hand/finger placed on a suspected driver) to confirm a reactive pair, Level 3 refines the process into direct muscle-to-muscle testing: testing the suspected driver muscle's own strength directly, then testing the suspected receiver, and comparing the pattern of weakness/strength shifts without relying purely on TL contact. This is combined with refined MMT positioning in supine, prone, seated, and standing postures to eliminate false substitution.

Advanced Multi-Phasic Gait Dynamics

Level 3 dissects gait into its component phases and tests each contralateral/diagonal coupling independently:

Gait PhaseWhat Is Assessed
Heel Strike / Loading ResponseEccentric control handoff from ankle dorsiflexors to hip/glute stabilizers
Single-Limb (Mid) StanceLateral Subsystem integrity — frontal-plane pelvic control
Terminal Stance / PropulsionPosterior chain and DLS force transfer into push-off
Swing Phase / Cross-Body CouplingContralateral arm-leg (and diagonal) synchrony — e.g., right shoulder extension paired with left hip extension

Beyond straight-plane gait, Level 3 also examines rotational and diagonal patterns (e.g., bringing the right arm toward the left knee) and frontal-plane variants (humeral/femoral abduction-adduction gait patterns), which are essential for sport-specific movements that a simple flexion/extension gait screen would miss entirely.

Extraocular Muscles & Oculo-Cervical "Direction of Dysfunction" (DOD)

The eyes' extraocular muscles are innervated by three cranial nerves and reflexively couple to cervical and postural tone through the vestibulo-ocular and cervico-ocular reflex arcs:

Cranial NerveMuscle(s)Primary Gaze Vector
CN III (Oculomotor)Superior Rectus, Inferior Rectus, Medial Rectus, Inferior ObliqueUp, down, adduction (in and up/down)
CN IV (Trochlear)Superior ObliqueDown and in (depression while adducted)
CN VI (Abducens)Lateral RectusAbduction (out)

Direction of Dysfunction (DOD) testing has the patient direct their gaze to each of the 6–8 cardinal positions while an indicator muscle is retested; the specific gaze vector that reproduces inhibition/facilitation pinpoints which extraocular muscle (and by extension which suboccipital/cervical relationship) is the true driver — critical in post-concussive, vestibular, and chronic neck-pain cases.

Hyoid, Deglutition & Tongue Stabilizers

The hyoid bone is a floating anchor point for swallowing (deglutition), tongue position, and anterior cervical stability — dysfunction here often hides behind chronic anterior neck tension or TMJ complaints.

  • Suprahyoid group: Digastric, Mylohyoid, Geniohyoid, Stylohyoid (elevate hyoid/depress mandible).
  • Infrahyoid group: Sternohyoid, Sternothyroid, Thyrohyoid, Omohyoid (depress/stabilize hyoid).
  • Tongue stabilizers: Genioglossus and associated intrinsic/extrinsic tongue musculature, tested for their contribution to swallow-pattern stability.

As with the eyes, the hyoid complex is assessed with Direction of Dysfunction testing — gentle manual vectoring of the hyoid in different directions while retesting an indicator muscle — to isolate exactly which suprahyoid/infrahyoid relationship is driving distant inhibition.

Scars: 8-Vector Protocol, Vector of Release & DOD

Scar tissue (surgical or traumatic) can become a chronic neurological driver, often testing hypersensitive to the touch even years after healing. Level 3 formalizes scar assessment:

  1. 8-Vector Mapping: the scar is challenged/therapy-localized in eight directions around its axis — North, South, East, West, Clockwise, Counter-Clockwise, Compression, and Distraction/Pinch — while an indicator muscle is retested in each vector.
  2. Vector of Release: whichever vector(s) reproduce the strongest inhibition/facilitation shift indicate the direction manual scar mobilization should be applied to normalize the distant muscle.
  3. Direction of Dysfunction (DOD): the same directional-testing logic used for eyes and hyoid, applied to the scar itself.
  4. Scar-to-Scar relationships: in patients with multiple scars (e.g., an appendectomy scar plus a C-section scar), one scar may be TL'd against another to find inter-scar driver relationships.

Common clinical targets: Cesarean-section scars, appendectomy scars, arthroscopic portal scars, umbilical/navel adhesions, and episiotomy scars — frequently implicated in postpartum core weakness, chronic low-back pain, and pelvic floor dysfunction.

Precaution: Scars less than 6–8 weeks old should only receive light superficial sensory challenge (never aggressive deep friction), and active/open wounds are an absolute contraindication for direct scar work.

Pelvic & Peripheral Ligamentous Reflexes (Ligamento-Muscular Reflex)

Ligaments are richly innervated with mechanoreceptors and can reflexively facilitate or inhibit muscles that cross the same joint — the ligamento-muscular reflex. Level 3's "Pelvic Ligaments and DOD" module, along with broader NKT ligament work, covers:

LigamentRegionCommonly Linked Muscle(s)
Sacrotuberous LigamentPelvis/SacrumBiceps Femoris, Gluteus Maximus (part of the DLS)
Sacrospinous / Iliolumbar LigamentLumbosacral/PelvisQuadratus Lumborum, deep lumbar stabilizers
Sacroiliac Joint (SIJ) Ligamentous ComplexPelvisGluteus Maximus, Lat Dorsi (via POS), pelvic floor
Alar LigamentsUpper cervical (C1-C2-Occiput)Suboccipitals, SCM, deep neck flexors
Anterior Talofibular (ATFL) & Calcaneofibular (CFL)AnklePeroneals, Gluteus Medius (distant kinetic-chain inhibition after ankle sprain)

This explains a very common clinical finding: a patient with a history of ankle sprain (ATFL/CFL laxity) presenting years later with unexplained ipsilateral gluteus medius inhibition and hip/low-back pain, with the ankle itself asymptomatic.

Reverse Therapy Localization (Reverse TL)

Introduced into the NKT Symposium/Masters curriculum by instructor Thomas Wells, Reverse TL solves the opposite problem from standard TL: the patient has an obvious, acutely painful, hypertonic muscle, but the clinician cannot yet identify what distant muscle is actually inhibited.

  1. Patient touches/localizes the painful, hypertonic driver.
  2. Clinician scans and tests a series of normally-strong prime movers across the kinetic chain.
  3. When a normally strong indicator muscle suddenly breaks weak while the patient touches the painful driver, the true inhibited receiver has been uncovered.
  4. Release the painful driver, then activate the newly identified inhibited receiver — same activation logic as the standard protocol.

Master Glossary (A–Z)

Consolidated terminology spanning all three levels — useful as a rapid pre-exam refresher.

Active TL

The patient actively contracts or touches the suspected driver tissue themselves (vs. the clinician applying it) while an indicator muscle is retested.

Anticipatory Postural Adjustment (APA)

A feed-forward postural bracing response generated just before a voluntary movement or an incoming perturbation.

Compensation Pattern

A substitute movement/muscle-recruitment strategy the MCC adopts after an injury, weakness, or repeated fault to still achieve the movement goal.

Direction of Dysfunction (DOD)

Testing multiple directions/vectors of a structure (eye gaze, hyoid position, scar pull) to isolate exactly which vector reproduces the inhibitory/facilitatory relationship.

Facilitated

Neurologically overactive; the muscle tests with a crisp, rigid "lock" and is readily/excessively recruited by the MCC.

Inhibited

Neurologically underactive; the muscle "gives" or fails under isometric test load because the MCC is not calling on it appropriately in this pattern.

Ligamento-Muscular Reflex

A reflex arc in which ligamentous mechanoreceptors facilitate or inhibit muscles crossing the same joint.

Locked Muscle

A muscle held in a protective, guarded contraction that cannot be accurately tested until it is first manually "unlocked."

Motor Control Center (MCC)

The cerebellar network that stores, coordinates, and automates learned multi-joint movement programs ("motor engrams").

Neuroedge

The maximum limit of successful correction achievable within a single treatment session before returns diminish or new compensations emerge.

Neurolock

A muscle test result showing a rock-solid, unmistakably strong neural connection with zero give.

Neuroplastic Window

The acute ~30–60 second period after a driver is released during which corrective isometric activation of the inhibited receiver most effectively reprograms the MCC.

Passive TL

The clinician (not the patient) applies contact/pressure to the suspected driver tissue while an indicator muscle is retested.

Reactive (Muscle) Pair

Two muscles in a confirmed driver–receiver relationship: releasing one immediately restores strength in the other.

Reciprocal Inhibition

A chronically tight/overactive muscle reflexively inhibits its direct antagonist (Sherrington's Law).

Strong in the Clear (SIC)

A muscle tests strong in isolation, with no therapy localization applied — confirms genuine facilitation.

Synergistic Dominance

A synergist muscle chronically substitutes for and outworks an inhibited prime mover within the same movement pattern.

Therapy Localization (TL)

Applying contact/sensory stimulation to a suspected driver tissue and immediately retesting an indicator muscle to confirm a neurological relationship.

Vector of Release

The specific directional line of manual mobilization (commonly for scars) shown by testing to normalize a distant inhibited muscle.

Weak in the Clear (WIC)

A muscle tests weak in isolation, with no interference/TL applied — confirms genuine inhibition rather than a masked substitution.

Zone of Apposition (ZOA)

The region where the diaphragm's dome directly opposes the inner rib cage; restoring it supports optimal length-tension and intra-abdominal pressure generation.

Clinical Protocol Cheat-Sheet

A condensed, session-ready version of the full NKT corrective algorithm for quick reference across all levels.

=================================================================================================== THE NKT CORRECTIVE ALGORITHM — QUICK REFERENCE =================================================================================================== STEP 1 — BASELINE TEST Position target muscle in a shortened, isolated vector. Apply a 2-second isometric ramp. Confirm the muscle is genuinely INHIBITED (Weak in the Clear). STEP 2 — CHALLENGE / THERAPY LOCALIZATION (TL) Apply sensory stimulation (touch, rub, or patient self-contact) to the hypothesized driver. STEP 3 — IMMEDIATE RETEST (< 2 seconds) Still weak -> hypothesis rejected, test the next candidate driver. Rock solid -> REACTIVE PAIR CONFIRMED. Continue to Step 4. STEP 4 — RELEASE THE FACILITATED DRIVER Soft tissue release / ischemic compression / IASTM / stretch for 30-60 seconds. STEP 5 — ACTIVATE THE INHIBITED RECEIVER Within the 30-60s neuroplastic window: isolated isometric activation, 3-5 reps x 5-second holds at 50-70% effort. STEP 6 — RETEST IN THE CLEAR Receiver must now test Strong in the Clear (no TL needed). STEP 7 — FUNCTIONAL REPATTERNING & HOME PROGRAM Non-negotiable order: 1) Release driver FIRST -> 2) Activate receiver SECOND. Frequency: 2-3x daily for 2-4 weeks for synaptic consolidation. ===================================================================================================

Written Case-Study Documentation Checklist

Required elements for NKT certification case studies at every level (per official certification FAQ):

  • How Therapy Localization (TL) was used
  • Whether Strong-in-the-Clear or Weak-in-the-Clear testing was applied (and why, if one was not applicable — e.g., testing a ligament rather than a muscle)
  • The process used to identify the facilitated and inhibited structures
  • The corrective technique performed on the driver
  • The results of retesting the receiver
  • The home exercise / corrective homework assigned

Case study counts required: Level 1 = 3, Level 2 = 6, Level 3 = 5, plus a live or Zoom-supervised session with an approved Certifier.

Clinical Safety, Red Flags & Contraindications

  • Absolute red flags (refer out immediately): progressive bilateral neurological deficits, saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder incontinence (cauda equina syndrome), acute unhealed fracture, fever with spinal tenderness (infectious discitis/osteomyelitis), active deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
  • Relative precautions: surgical scars <6–8 weeks old (light superficial challenge only, no deep friction), active open wounds, severe osteoporosis (calibrate MMT force downward).
Level 1 Review

Level 1 Practice Questions

Q1. What does a "spongy give" during manual muscle testing indicate?
The muscle's neurological availability under isometric load.
Answer: The muscle is Inhibited — the MCC is not recruiting it properly for this pattern, even if the muscle is anatomically capable of producing force elsewhere.
Q2. TFL dominance over Gluteus Medius is an example of which compensation pattern?
Consider whether TFL and Glute Med are agonist/synergist or true antagonists.
Answer: Synergistic Dominance — TFL is a hip-abduction synergist that substitutes for and outworks the true prime mover, Gluteus Medius.
Q3. Why does NKT use a binary Facilitated/Inhibited result instead of the 0–5 Kendall scale?
Think about what NKT is actually measuring.
Answer: NKT tests rate coding and recruitment latency (a neurological availability question), not raw contractile strength, so a graded strength scale doesn't capture what's being assessed.
Q4. A tight Psoas reciprocally inhibiting the Gluteus Maximus is an example of what?
Are Psoas and Glute Max synergists or antagonists at the hip?
Answer: Reciprocal Inhibition — Psoas (hip flexor) and Glute Max (hip extensor) are direct antagonists; per Sherrington's Law, an overactive Psoas reflexively inhibits Glute Max.
Q5. What joint starting position minimizes passive tissue interference during MMT?
Think about where non-contractile tension is lowest.
Answer: A shortened / inner-to-mid range starting position.

Level 2 Practice Questions

Q1. Which subsystem links the Latissimus Dorsi to the contralateral Gluteus Maximus?
Which sling crosses the body diagonally through the thoracolumbar fascia?
Answer: The Posterior Oblique Subsystem (POS).
Q2. What does testing an indicator muscle on an inhale-hold vs. exhale-hold reveal?
Which structure is being challenged by two different breath states?
Answer: Whether the diaphragm/respiratory pattern is acting as a hidden driver of the muscle's inhibition.
Q3. The Anterior Oblique Subsystem is most classically implicated in which condition?
Think rotational trunk-to-groin force transfer in athletes.
Answer: Athletic pubalgia ("sports hernia") and adductor/groin overload.
Q4. What is the Zone of Apposition (ZOA)?
Relates to the diaphragm's mechanical relationship with the rib cage.
Answer: The area where the diaphragm's dome directly opposes the inner rib cage, important for optimal length-tension and intra-abdominal pressure generation.
Q5. Testing a muscle under manual joint compression vs. decompression evaluates which type of receptor input?
Not muscle spindles — think capsule.
Answer: Joint capsule mechanoreceptors, which respond differently to compressive vs. distractive loading.
Level 3 Review

Level 3 Practice Questions

Q1. What are the 8 vectors used in the scar assessment protocol?
Think compass directions plus rotational and pressure vectors.
Answer: North, South, East, West, Clockwise, Counter-Clockwise, Compression, and Distraction/Pinch.
Q2. Which cranial nerve innervates the Superior Oblique, and what gaze direction does it primarily control?
This is the only extraocular muscle innervated by this nerve.
Answer: CN IV (Trochlear) — controls downward gaze while the eye is adducted (looking down and in).
Q3. In Reverse Therapy Localization, what does the clinician look for?
The patient already knows what hurts — what is the clinician hunting for?
Answer: A normally-strong indicator muscle that suddenly breaks weak while the patient touches the painful, hypertonic driver — revealing the true inhibited receiver.
Q4. A patient with an old ATFL/CFL ankle sprain later develops unexplained ipsilateral hip weakness. What mechanism explains this?
Think about mechanoreceptor-rich connective tissue, not muscle.
Answer: The ligamento-muscular reflex — lax/altered ankle ligament mechanoreceptors reflexively inhibit distant muscles such as Gluteus Medius.
Q5. What distinguishes Muscle-to-Muscle Testing from standard Therapy Localization?
Consider what physically contacts the driver in each method.
Answer: Muscle-to-Muscle Testing directly tests the suspected driver muscle's own strength and compares it to the receiver's pattern, rather than relying on a hand/finger TL contact.
Q6. Which ligament is part of the Deep Longitudinal Subsystem chain?
Connects the hamstring to the erector spinae fascia.
Answer: The Sacrotuberous Ligament.
